The big picture

What we are building

The system looks at a parking lot and decides, for every spot, one of three answers: taken, free or unknown. It shows that on a phone within a few seconds. It gets its answers from four kinds of evidence. We trust them in this order.

1 · Camera

Strongest and automatic. A camera looks at the lot. A computer vision model (software that finds objects in pictures) finds the cars. We compare each car with the spot shapes we drew in advance. Nobody has to do anything. The weakness is that it cannot see around corners, and it struggles at night or in heavy rain.

2 · Gate counts

Accurate in total, blind to location. Counters at the entrances of each aisle or bay note every car that comes in or goes out. "Cars in minus cars out" tells you how many cars are inside, even where no camera can see. If the cameras account for 76 cars and the counters say 93 are inside, the other 17 are probably in hidden spots (Lab 4). The weakness is drift: small counting mistakes pile up, so the count must be reset now and then.

3 · People

Useful, but they fade. A student taps "Row C has a free spot" or "Row C is full". When a hidden spot is still uncertain, the app can also ask someone nearby a question: "Is the spot behind the pillar free?". Because people forget and sometimes get it wrong, every answer loses weight as it gets older, and the camera always wins an argument if it is fresh.

4 · Patterns

Weakest, a guess, and built last. From past data we learn that Tuesday at 9:45 is usually packed. This is the only evidence that works when the camera is off and nobody has reported anything.

What the MVP deliberately does not do

No hardware sensors, no user accounts, no iPhone or Android app, no navigation, no payments, no real campus pilot at first, no heavy model training, and no automatic discovery of spots from painted lines. Each of these is a good idea and a time sink. Leaving them out is how a project finishes.

Hands-on

Four ideas you can play with

These are the four bits of logic that most people find confusing. They happen in this order inside the system: find the car, avoid flickering, mix the evidence, then use the gate count to estimate what no camera can see.

Lab 1 · Is that car in this spot?

The detector draws a box around each car. We draw a shape around each spot. To decide whether a spot is taken, we measure how much of the spot the car box covers. This is called overlap (the formal version, which also divides by the car's own area, is called IoU, intersection over union). Drag the car and move the threshold.

Car covers 0% of A-07 taken

Low threshold: a car in the next spot leans over the line and marks this one taken by mistake. High threshold: a car parked crooked still counts as free. Slide the car to the edge to see both. Real spots are slanted shapes, so the code uses a geometry library called shapely for the maths. When one car overlaps two spots, give it to the spot with the larger overlap.

Lab 2 · Why the app must not trust one picture

The camera checks a spot every 5 seconds. A car drives past (frames 6 and 7), and the model misses the parked car a few times later. If the app changes its answer on every reading, the colour flickers and nobody trusts it. The fix is hysteresis: only change the answer after the new reading repeats M times in a row. A filled box means taken. An empty box means free. A red bar under a box means that box is wrong.

What the app shows

Try M = 1: it is fast but flickers. Try M = 2: still fooled by two bad readings in a row. Try M = 6: it is calm but slow to notice that a car left. Your job is to pick the value that a driver would tolerate. About 30 seconds of delay is fine for people. A few wrong flips is not.

Lab 3 · Mixing the camera with what people say

Each piece of evidence is a vote. Its strength is its starting weight multiplied by a fade factor, e^(−age ÷ τ). Here τ ("tau") is the time scale: after one τ the weight has dropped to about 37%. The camera votes strongly and fades over minutes. A person's report votes weakly and fades faster. We add the votes up and turn the total into a probability (that is what the "sigmoid" does). Set up a situation and see who wins.

Try this: camera says taken, a fresh report says free. The camera wins. Now make the camera reading 8 minutes old, or set it to offline. The report takes over. That is the whole idea of fusion. These weights (4, 1.5, and the time scales 3 and 8 minutes) are starting guesses. You will tune them with the simulator in feature F7.

Lab 4 · What the gate count says about hidden spots

Some spots sit behind pillars or trees, so no camera sees them. The gate counters still know how many cars came in minus how many went out. If the cameras account for most of those cars, the rest must be in the hidden spots. It is the same bookkeeping as counting heads against seats in a cinema. Change the numbers and watch the estimate move.

Try this: raise the counting error to 10 and the answer turns into "not sure". That is the moment the app should ask someone nearby. Also remember that the count only means something relative to a known starting point (a baseline), and that small mistakes pile up over time, so the baseline must be reset now and then.

Accuracy = right answers out of all. Precision = when it says free, how often it is truly free (a low value sends people to a taken spot). Recall = of all truly free spots, how many it found (a low value hides the empty spot, which is your original problem). The lazy detector scores 80% accuracy and 0% recall. That is why we report recall.

Targets to aim for

These are hypotheses to confirm or revise after your first evaluation run. They are not promises, and the first honest result might be lower.

  • Free-spot recall: 90% or better on daytime images.
  • Per-spot accuracy: 90% or better, reported separately for sunny, cloudy and rainy.
  • Update delay: under about 30 seconds from a car leaving to the app showing free. This is set by the M-in-a-row rule, not by compute.
  • API p95 response: under 200 ms for reading the lot state.
  • Stale data: any spot with no fresh evidence for 2 minutes shows "unknown".
  • Gate count: within a few cars of the truth after a 10-minute test video, with the drift rate measured and reported.

Where to get test data

  • PKLot and CNRPark+EXT: public datasets of parking-lot images with every spot labelled free or occupied. Check each licence and size before downloading, and credit them in the README. They come from other countries and camera angles, so say so in your limits.
  • Several different dataset cameras, to build the pretend campus.
  • Traffic or road-camera footage with a visible entrance, for the gate counter.
  • A short clip you film yourself, for reality.
  • Toy cars on a printed grid, for a controlled demo.
Putting it together

How the pieces connect

Data flows left to right. Click any box to see what goes in, what comes out, how it breaks, and how you test it. One design choice matters: the detector is kept "dumb". It only reports what it saw. All the thinking (smoothing, mixing reports, fading) lives in one place on the server, so there is a single source of truth.

The data model

Lot (the whole VIT main parking)
 └─ Bay (one section of it)
     ├─ Camera   (each has its own spot file)
     │   └─ Spot (main camera, optional backup)
     └─ Counting line (in or out, at an aisle or bay entrance)

Spot state = free | taken | unknown + confidence + last confirmed
Bay total  = sum of its spots + an estimate for hidden spots

Where does the detector run?

YOLO needs real CPU power, and free hosting is weak. The practical answer is to run the workers (one per camera) on your laptop (or a small rented machine) and let it send the light JSON messages to a hosted API. The public demo can also replay a recorded observation file, so a recruiter can open it at any time without your laptop on. This is called edge inference: the heavy work happens near the camera, and only small results travel.

Directions

Three ways to spend your hours

All three use the same core. They differ in where you invest the extra effort, and so in the story you tell afterwards. Pick one, or take A and borrow a small piece of C.

Things you will be able to say in interviews

  • Why SSE and not WebSockets? Updates only flow server to browser. SSE does that over plain HTTP and the browser reconnects by itself, so there is less to build and fewer things to break.
  • How do you deal with bad data? Hysteresis against flicker, a time limit after which evidence expires into "unknown", and a rule that fresh camera data beats old reports.
  • How did you measure it, and what did you choose to report? Recall of free spots, split by weather, because missing an empty spot is the exact problem users have.
  • What breaks at 100 lots? Detection cost grows with cameras (batch it, or use a queue and several workers). Database writes grow too (store only changes, and delete old observations). Fan-out to many phones grows (cache the current state, and partition by lot).
  • What about privacy? Frames are processed in memory and thrown away. Only slot states leave the worker. No plates, no faces, no stored video.

Build order and checklist

Each phase ends with something that runs.

Week 1

Your first week

The goal is small on purpose. By the end of the week, one command prints a table showing how well a pretrained detector finds free and taken spots on real parking-lot images. There is no server, database or map yet. This is the first part of Phase 1, about 6 hours in four sessions, and it tests the biggest risk first: can the detector see the spots?

Time

Four sessions of 60 to 105 minutes. Put them in your calendar today, for example two weekday evenings and the weekend.

You will have

A repo, real images, correct spot shapes, a detector that colours each spot, and your first accuracy table.

Not this week

No React, no database, no map, no model training, no camera permissions, and no tuning beyond one threshold.

Before session 1 (10 minutes)

  • Check that you have Python 3.11 or newer and about 10 GB of free disk space.
  • Create an empty private repo on GitHub. You can make it public later.
  • Block the four sessions in your calendar. A session that is not scheduled usually does not happen.
