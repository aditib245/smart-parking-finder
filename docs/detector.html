Block 3 · in plain English

How the app decides a spot is taken

A spotter looks at the photo and draws a box around every car. Then we lay a stencil of the parking spots on top. If a car's box covers at least half of a spot, that spot is taken.

That's the whole idea. Everything below explains each piece, one at a time.

1

The whole journey of one photo

Think of it as an assembly line. A photo goes in on the left. Each station does one small job and passes its result to the right.

2

The spotter is YOLO, and we didn't train it

YOLO is a program that has already looked at hundreds of thousands of labelled photos and learned what cars, people, dogs and 77 other things look like. We download its "brain" (a 20 MB file called yolo26s.pt) and use it as it is.

Analogy

Hiring an experienced security guard instead of raising a baby and teaching them what a car is. The guard already knows. We just tell them: "Look at this screen and point at every car."

3

What the spotter hands back

For every object it notices, YOLO gives three things: where it is (a box), what it thinks it is (as a number, e.g. 2 means car), and how sure it is (0 to 1). It hands them back as three separate lists, like three columns torn off a receipt.

The first item of each list belongs together, then the second, and so on. zip lines them up into rows; the filter keeps cars, buses and trucks that are sure enough.

That filtering is the keep_vehicles function. It's kept apart from YOLO on purpose, so a test can feed it made-up rows and check the keep/drop rules in a split second, without loading the 20 MB brain.

4

"How sure is sure enough?" Try it on a real photo

This is the real PUCPR photo with YOLO's real guesses. The slider is the cut-off: YOLO's guesses below it are ignored. Slide it and watch spots change colour.

Analogy

A teacher taking attendance by asking "Who's here?" If they only count the loud answers (high cut-off), the quiet kids at the back get marked absent. White and silver cars on bright concrete are the quiet kids: YOLO sees them but only mumbles "maybe a car, 15% sure".

5

Two safety nets, one after the other

A low cut-off sounds risky: we're now listening to guesses YOLO is only 10% sure about. But a guess has to pass two checks before it can mark a spot as taken.

On this photo, even at the lowest setting no empty spot was wrongly called taken. On rainy or night photos that may not hold. Block 4 checks it on all 12,400 photos before we trust 0.10.

6

Analogy

A restaurant app. Saying "no table" when one is free (phantom) loses a customer quietly. Saying "table free" when it isn't (missed) sends someone across town to a full restaurant. That's why we lowered the cut-off: it cuts missed cars, the worse mistake.

7

Why CarDetector is a class

Until now every piece of code was a plain function. The detector is a class because it has a slow setup step that we only want to do once.

Analogy

A coffee machine. Heating it up takes a while; after that each cup is quick. CarDetector() heats the machine. .detect(photo) pours a cup. self is the machine talking about itself: "my water tank, my settings". It's the same as this in JavaScript.

8

Who talks to whom

Each file has one job. The spotter and the ruler never talk to each other. One "manager" file asks each of them for its part and puts the answers together.

Like your frontend work

It's a page component calling two separate API helpers and merging the results. detector.py knows photos and boxes, nothing about spots. geometry.py knows shapes, nothing about photos. Keeping them apart means we can swap YOLO for a better model later without touching the overlap maths.
